Shift Fillers — Company Brief & Deal Angles

Prep for a CEO-level conversation about partnership or acquisition. Who they are, who actually controls them, where the models overlap with Gopher, and how sessions 1–4 findings shape the pitch.
The single most important fact before the call
Shift Fillers is majority-owned by Raise (Toronto — the former Ian Martin Group, founded 1957, North America's only B Corp staffing firm) since August 2023. Any acquisition conversation is ultimately a conversation with Raise — whose CEO is now Emily Schmidt (Tim Masson, who signed the Shiftfillers deal, moved to Cofounder & Advisor). A partnership conversation can start with Shift Fillers' own leadership, but they cannot sell the company without Raise.

Shift Fillers (legally Shift Fillers LLC, brand also written "Shiftfillers") is a tech-forward temporary-staffing firm. It was built by people who tried to disrupt this exact industry before: Roderick Smyth founded TempBuddy (acquired by Bullhorn), Janeesa Hollingshead ran expansion at Uber Works (Uber's shuttered gig-staffing arm), and Drew Golin owned InterSolutions, a large apartment-staffing agency. The founding thesis — mobile-first apps for workers and employers, automated onboarding, same-day/weekly pay, layered on a traditional W-2 employment spine — is essentially "gig-economy UX on a staffing-agency chassis." Their stack runs on Avionté (ATS/payroll) with their own mobile front end.

Similarities to Gopher — and the differences that matter

Where the models rhyme

  • Same core problem: matching a flexible labor supply to fluctuating demand, fast. Their "fill every shift" = our "fill every order."
  • Mobile-first worker experience: app-based onboarding, shift management, and matching — the same bet Gopher 4.0 is making.
  • Speed-to-work as the funnel metric: they brag about apply-to-work in under a week; session 4 showed Gopher's washout curve is exactly this problem (43% never make a second delivery).
  • Fast pay as retention: weekly pay prominently marketed — the same lever behind Gopher's payout-speed thinking.
  • Referral engine: award-winning worker-referral bonus program; mirrors the supply-first playbook in the Marketing field guide.
  • Founder DNA: Uber Works + TempBuddy alumni — they explicitly built "gig UX" onto staffing. Culturally they speak Gopher's language.

Where they diverge

  • W-2 employer, not a marketplace: workers are employees with ACA health plans, dental, vision, life, disability. Gopher's workers are independent contractors. Blending the two raises classification questions — a real diligence item, not a footnote.
  • B2B demand, not B2C: their customer is a warehouse or food plant buying recurring shifts; Gopher's is a consumer buying one task. Different sales motion, margins, and cash cycle (staffing carries payroll float).
  • Shift-based, scheduled work vs. Gopher's on-demand tasks. Their workers want 40 predictable hours; many wouldn't want gig scraps — but the ones who don't get placed would.
  • Not independent: majority-owned by a 69-year-old Canadian staffing group. Strategy, and any sale, runs through Raise.

Why they're interesting to Gopher specifically

Sessions 3–4 established Gopher's core disease: demand grew 28% while the active fleet shrank 32%; 6,346 orders expired unfilled in 12 months; the washout curve kills new gophers before their third delivery because work volume arrives too slowly. Shift Fillers is, structurally, a machine for the exact things Gopher is missing:

  • A recruiting-and-activation engine. They industrialized apply → screen → onboard → working within days, at national scale. That pipeline pointed at Gopher's top-20 gap markets is worth more than any ad spend.
  • A bench with idle capacity. Staffing agencies always carry unplaced and between-assignment workers. Those are pre-screened, background-checked, work-ready people — the profile Gopher struggles to source. (Their bench is in industrial corridors; overlap with Gopher's gap markets — Philadelphia is ~15 miles from their Moorestown HQ, a session-3 top-5 gap market.)
  • Retention infrastructure Gopher lacks. Benefits, weekly payroll, and human recruiters who call you back — the antidotes to the $6/month-median-earnings churn found in session 4, if the economics can carry them.
  • The reverse flow works too. Gopher's 30k+ registered-but-dormant signups (706 in Oklahoma alone, 12k in NC) are a candidate database a staffing firm would pay for — a monetizable asset in a partnership, with proper consent.

Be prepared for the conversation to invert — Raise acquiring or investing in Gopher's platform is the likelier M&A shape given relative sizes ($14M+ revenue, 450+ employee parent vs. Gopher pre-scale).

Talking points & questions for the call

  • Open on the shared thesis: both companies exist because labor matching is broken; they fixed it for scheduled industrial shifts, Gopher for on-demand consumer tasks. The models are complementary, not competitive — no client overlap, heavy worker-profile overlap.
  • Bring one number: 6,346 unfilled orders in 12 months, concentrated in ~20 metros — several in their operating footprint. "What would your engine charge to fill that?"
  • Ask about bench utilization: what % of their active roster is unplaced in a given week? (Their pain = our supply.)
  • Ask where Raise sits: "How does the Raise relationship shape what you can do independently?" — polite way to learn who signs what.
  • Ask about Uber Works lessons: Hollingshead lived through Uber's failed staffing play; whoever's on the call will have views on why pure-gig industrial staffing failed. Their answer is free diligence on Gopher's own model.
  • Do not lead with acquisition. Lead with the Philadelphia pilot; let them raise structure.

Method & caveats

  • Financials are self-reported and stale: the $14M / +250% figures are 2021–2022, pre-Raise. No post-2023 revenue is public; PitchBook/PrivCo profiles exist but are paywalled.
  • CEO status is the one open question — Golin's LinkedIn points to SpotSync; verify current titles via LinkedIn or a direct ask before naming him in outreach. Staffing Industry Analysts articles that likely cover post-deal people moves returned HTTP 403.
  • "Founded 2020" (site) vs "launched April 2021" (their blog) — company formed late 2020, platform live spring 2021.
  • Case studies (May–June 2026) are anonymized with no published metrics — ask for real numbers on the call.
